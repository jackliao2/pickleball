The third-shot drop

The shot that separates beginners from everyone else: why it is necessary, how to hit it, and when to drive instead. Updated 21 September 2026.

Ask any coach what one shot they would teach a new doubles player and the answer is the third-shot drop. It is not glamorous. Done well it looks like nothing, a soft ball that floats over the net and dies in the kitchen. But it is the shot that gets the serving team from the worst position on the court to an even one, and the shot that, more than any other, decides whether you are a 3.0 or a 3.5.

Why the third shot is a problem

Count the shots. The serve is one. The return is two. The serving team’s reply is three. By the two-bounce rule the serving team must let the return bounce, which keeps them at the baseline while the receiver, having hit the return, walks up to join their partner at the kitchen line. So at the moment the third shot is hit, the geometry is this: two receivers at the net, 7 feet from it, paddles up; two servers 40 feet away, about to hit a ball that has bounced near their baseline.

If the serving team drives that ball, it arrives at two ready players who volley it back at their feet, and the servers are now hitting up from the transition zone, which is worse. If they lob it, it gets smashed. There is one shot that does not lose the point immediately: a soft ball that lands in the kitchen, cannot be volleyed, and bounces low enough that the receivers have to lift it. While that ball is in the air, the serving team moves forward. That is the third-shot drop, and it exists because the two-bounce rule and the kitchen rule together make it the only sensible option.

What a good one looks like

  • It lands in the kitchen, ideally in the front half of it, near the opponents’ feet. Landing past the kitchen line means it can be volleyed and you have hit a slow drive.
  • Its apex is on your side of the net. The ball is descending as it crosses, so it keeps descending and is below net height by the time it can be hit.
  • It has enough height to be safe. From the baseline you are 22 feet from the net; the ball needs to clear it by 1 to 3 feet, more than a dink, because it has further to travel and you have less margin.
  • It is unhurried. The ball should be slow enough that you can take two or three steps forward before it is hit.

A drop does not need to be a winner. It needs to be unattackable. If the opponents hit it back softly into your kitchen, the drop did its job: you are now at, or nearly at, the line and the point is a dink rally that started even.

Technique

Get behind the ball

The most common cause of a bad drop is hitting it on the move, reaching forward as you run in. Get set first. Read the return, move to where it will bounce, stop, and hit from a balanced stance with your weight slightly forward. You will move in after the shot, not during it.

Low to high, from the shoulder

It is a lift, the same motion as a dink but longer. Open paddle face, short backswing, contact out in front and below waist height, a smooth low-to-high path with the follow-through finishing toward your target. Very little wrist. The power comes from the length of the lift, not from the arm speed; think of tossing the ball underhand into a basket at the far kitchen line.

Let it drop

Take the ball after the top of the bounce, as it is falling, at knee height or a little above. A ball taken on the rise is hard to soften. Bend your knees; a drop hit from an upright stance almost always floats.

Target the middle first

Aim for the middle of the far kitchen, over the lowest part of the net. It is the safest target, the hardest for two opponents to claim, and a miss to either side is still in. Once the middle drop is reliable, drops to the backhand side of the weaker opponent are the upgrade.

Then move

A drop with no movement behind it is wasted. As the ball leaves the paddle, start forward. If the drop is good, keep going to the line. If it is a little high, stop in the transition zone in a ready position and block the attack softly into the kitchen (a reset), then continue. If it is really bad, stop and defend. Never sprint blindly to the line behind a ball you have not looked at.

Drop or drive?

The drop is the default, not the law. There are good reasons to drive the third ball:

SituationBetter choice
Return is deep and lowDrop. A drive from a low ball has to be lifted and sits up.
Return is short and sits upDrive. Take the free ball and follow it in.
Opponents have slow hands or paddle downDrive at the body, then drop the fifth.
Opponents block well and reset everythingDrop. Driving just feeds them.
Only one opponent is at the lineDrive at the one who is back, or drop to the one at the line and move in.
Windy or outdoors with a fast ballDrive more; drops float in wind.
You are tired or off balanceDrop crosscourt, deep in the kitchen, and reset.

A common intermediate pattern is drive, then drop: a hard third shot that the opponents block back, followed by a soft fifth shot into the kitchen. The drive earns a slightly weaker ball; the drop cashes it in. Another is the shake and bake, where one partner drives and the other crashes the net to put away the pop-up.

The fifth-shot drop

Nobody hits a perfect third every time. When the drop is a bit high and gets attacked, or when you drove and it came back, the serving team is usually somewhere in the transition zone with a ball at their feet. The fifth shot is the second attempt at a soft ball: block or lift it gently into the kitchen, and now move to the line. Points at intermediate level are often decided by whether the serving team can stay calm through the third and fifth shots rather than by the quality of any one of them.

Mistakes

  1. Too high. Floats to chest height at the line and is smashed. Cause: upright stance, hitting on the rise, too much wrist.
  2. Too deep. Lands past the kitchen line and is volleyed. Cause: too much lift, hitting through the ball.
  3. Into the net. The over-correction. Cause: trying to hit a winner; a drop should clear by a foot or more.
  4. Running while hitting. Cause: impatience to get to the line. Set, hit, then go.
  5. Hitting every third the same way. If your opponents know a drop is coming, they lean in and take it early. Mix in a drive now and then to keep them honest.

Drills

Baseline drops to a target (alone or with a feeder)
Put a towel in the middle of the far kitchen. From the baseline, drop 20 balls at it after a self-feed or a partner’s feed. Count the ones that land in the kitchen and would have been below net height. Move the towel to each side.
Drop and advance (two people)
Partner at the kitchen line feeds a deep ball. You drop, move in three steps, receive the next ball in the transition zone, reset it into the kitchen, and continue to the line. Repeat until you reach the line without hitting a ball above the net.
Third-shot decision (four people)
Play points that start with a serve and return. The serving team must call “drop” or “drive” out loud before the third shot. Keep score of which choice wins the point. Most groups discover they drive far more often than they should.

Common questions

How high over the net should a drop go?
About 1 to 3 feet, with the apex on your side. Higher than a dink because it travels further.
Where should it land?
In the kitchen, ideally the front half, in the middle to start with.
Should I use topspin?
A little topspin makes the ball dip and is useful once the basic lift is reliable. Start flat.
Is the third-shot drop needed in singles?
Less so. With one opponent, a deep drive to the open side is often better. The drop is a doubles shot first.
Why do pros drive the third so often?
Because their drives are heavy and their fifth-shot drops are excellent. The drive-then-drop pattern is a two-shot version of the same idea.
