How to choose a pickleball paddle

What each spec on a paddle actually changes on court, what beginners need, and when an expensive paddle is worth it. No brands, no affiliate links. Updated 27 September 2026.

There are hundreds of pickleball paddles, the prices range from the cost of lunch to the cost of a phone, and the marketing is loud. The good news is that a paddle has only five specs that matter, each one changes the feel in a predictable way, and a beginner needs far less than the marketing suggests. This guide explains the five, what they do, and how to choose. It does not recommend brands or models, and this site earns nothing from paddle sales.

The rules a paddle has to follow

Under USA Pickleball rules, a paddle’s length plus width may not exceed 24 inches, and the length may not exceed 17 inches. There is no weight limit. The surface cannot have holes, rubber, or textures beyond a set roughness limit. Tournament play requires a paddle on USA Pickleball’s approved list; recreational play does not. Almost every paddle sold by a sports retailer is legal, but if you plan to play sanctioned events, check the list.

1. Weight

Paddles are grouped roughly like this:

ClassWeightFeel
LightUnder 7.5 ozFast hands, easy on the arm, more control, less power. Harder to keep stable against hard balls.
Mid-weight7.5–8.3 ozThe default. Balanced power and control. Most players end up here.
HeavyOver 8.3 ozMore power and stability, slower hands, more strain on the elbow and shoulder.

Weight is the spec you feel most. If you have any history of tennis elbow or shoulder trouble, lean lighter. If you are coming from tennis and want a paddle that feels solid on drives, mid-weight is the place to start. Lead tape can add weight later; you cannot take it away.

2. Shape and length

Because length plus width is capped, every paddle trades one for the other.

  • Standard (about 16 × 8 in): the widest face and the biggest sweet spot. Most forgiving, best for beginners and for kitchen play.
  • Elongated (about 16.5 × 7.5 in): more reach and more leverage for power and spin, narrower sweet spot. Popular with singles players and hard hitters.
  • Hybrid (about 16.3 × 7.8 in): in between.

Handle length is related: longer handles leave room for a two-handed backhand but shorten the face. If you do not hit two-handed, a standard handle gives you more face.

3. Core thickness

Almost all modern paddles have a polymer honeycomb core. What varies is thickness.

  • Thick (around 16 mm): softer, quieter, more control, larger effective sweet spot. The ball stays on the face slightly longer, which helps with dinks, drops and resets.
  • Thin (around 13 mm): more pop and power, more feedback, less forgiveness on off-centre hits.

For the soft game that decides most recreational doubles (see how to dink and the third-shot drop), a thicker core is the safer choice.

4. Face material

  • Fibreglass: more pop, less spin, usually cheaper. Good for players who want easy power.
  • Carbon fibre: more control, more consistent feel, better at gripping the ball for spin. The most common face on mid- and high-end paddles.
  • Hybrid and composite faces: combinations aiming for both.
  • Wood: heavy, cheap, durable. Fine for trying the sport; you will outgrow it quickly.

Many carbon paddles have a textured surface (“grit”) to add spin. It wears down with use, which is one reason a paddle that felt great a year ago feels flatter now.

5. Grip size

Grip circumference usually runs from about 4 to 4.5 inches. A grip that is too big tires the forearm and limits wrist movement at the kitchen line; one that is too small makes you squeeze too hard. A quick test: hold the paddle with a normal grip and you should be able to fit the index finger of your other hand between your fingertips and the heel of your palm. When in doubt, go smaller, since a thin overgrip can build it up.

What a beginner actually needs

A mid-weight, standard-shape paddle with a thick core and a comfortable grip. That is it. A mid-priced paddle with those specs will serve a new player well for a year or more. Expensive paddles offer more consistent feel, more spin and more durability, but those advantages matter much more to a 4.0 player than to someone learning the two-bounce rule. The biggest improvements at 2.5 to 3.5 come from strategy and the soft game, not equipment; the beginner strategy guide is a better investment than a premium paddle.

Choosing by play style

You are…Look for
New to the sportMid-weight, standard shape, thick core
A soft-game doubles playerThick core, carbon face, standard or hybrid shape
A hard hitter or singles playerElongated shape, thinner core, mid-to-heavy weight
Coming from tennisElongated or hybrid, mid-weight; resist the urge to go heavy
Nursing an elbow or shoulderLighter, thick core, larger sweet spot

Try before you buy

Most clubs and open-play sessions have players happy to let you hit a few balls with their paddle, and some shops run demo programmes. Hitting dinks and blocks for ten minutes tells you more than any spec sheet. Pay attention to how it feels on soft shots, not drives: almost every paddle feels good on a drive.

Care and lifespan

Keep paddles out of hot cars, which can damage the core. Replace the overgrip when it gets slick. Signs that a paddle is wearing out: a dead spot in the middle, a rattle, a new buzz on contact, or a face that has lost its texture. A regular player might replace a paddle every year or two.

Common questions

What weight pickleball paddle should a beginner use?
Mid-weight, roughly 7.5 to 8.3 ounces. It balances power and control and is gentle enough on the arm for long sessions.
Is a thicker paddle core better?
For control and the soft game, yes. A thick core (around 16 mm) is softer and more forgiving; a thin core (around 13 mm) gives more power and pop.
Carbon fibre or fibreglass?
Carbon fibre for control and spin, fibreglass for pop and power. Most mid- and high-end paddles use carbon.
Do expensive paddles make you better?
Not much at beginner and intermediate level. Strategy, footwork and the soft game matter far more. Expensive paddles help most at 4.0 and above.
How do I know my grip size?
Holding the paddle normally, you should be able to fit your other index finger between your fingertips and palm. If in doubt, choose the smaller size and add an overgrip.
How long does a pickleball paddle last?
For regular players, typically one to two years before the face loses texture or the core develops dead spots.
