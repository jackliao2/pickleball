The reset

The defensive shot that turns a hard ball at your feet into a soft ball in their kitchen, and the one that separates 3.5 from 4.0. Updated 27 September 2026.

Every point in pickleball eventually has a moment where you are losing it: you are stuck in the transition zone, a drive is coming at your feet, or an opponent has sped up at you at the line. Most players respond by hitting back harder, or by popping the ball up in panic. Good players do something else. They take the pace off the ball and drop it softly into the opponents’ kitchen, so that the rally goes back to neutral and nobody can attack. That is a reset, and it is the most important defensive shot in the game.

What a reset is

A reset is any soft shot hit from a defensive position that lands in the opponents’ kitchen and cannot be attacked. It is not a new kind of stroke. It is a dink or a drop, played in response to pace instead of as part of a slow exchange. The word describes its job: it resets the point to a neutral dink rally.

Why this matters: in a fast exchange, whoever is in the better position usually wins. If you can reliably take a hard ball and make it soft, you never have to win that exchange. You can simply opt out of it.

When to reset

  • In the transition zone. You are moving up after a third-shot drop that was a bit high, and the opponents drive at your feet. Stop, reset into the kitchen, then keep moving.
  • After a speed-up at the line. An opponent attacks a ball at your chest. Instead of counter-attacking, absorb it and drop it back short.
  • Against a banger. Their pace is exactly what makes a reset easy; you are just redirecting it. See how to beat bangers.
  • When you are off balance. Reaching wide, stretched, or late. A soft ball is always safer than a hard one from a bad position.
  • When the ball is below the net. You cannot attack a low ball safely, so the right answer is always soft.

Technique

Stop before you hit

This is the hardest part and the most important. If you are moving forward when you reset, your momentum adds pace to the ball and it floats. When you see the opponent about to hit, split-step: a small hop that lands you balanced on both feet, knees bent. Then hit. Resetting while running forward is how good intentions end up at the opponent’s chest.

Low and wide base

Get your eyes closer to the height of the ball. A wide stance and bent knees let you reach low balls with your legs instead of your arm. If you bend at the waist, the paddle face closes and the ball goes into the net.

Soft grip

Grip pressure around 2 or 3 out of 10. A loose grip lets the paddle give a little on contact, which absorbs pace. A tight grip turns the paddle into a wall and the ball rebounds at the speed it came in.

Short stroke, open face

Almost no backswing. The paddle meets the ball out in front of you with the face slightly open, and moves forward and up a few inches. Think of catching the ball on the paddle rather than hitting it. For a very hard ball, the motion is closer to a block with a slight lift.

Contact in front

If the ball gets beside or behind you, you lose control of the face angle. Meet it in front, where you can see both the ball and the paddle.

Where to aim

  • The kitchen, first. The target is not a corner; it is the whole kitchen. A reset that lands anywhere in the opponents’ non-volley zone has done its job.
  • The middle. It is over the lowest part of the net and gives the widest margin.
  • Away from the attacker. If one opponent has just sped up, reset toward their partner or crosscourt, so the attacker has to move to get it.

Depth control matters more than direction. A reset that lands past the kitchen line is a slow ball at an opponent’s waist, and they will attack it. When in doubt, aim shorter.

Reset from the transition zone

This is the classic case. You drop the third shot, move in, and the opponents attack it because it was a little high. You are 10 to 15 feet from the net with a ball coming at your feet. Split-step, bend, and lift the ball into the kitchen. Once it lands there, the opponents have to let it bounce, and you take another two or three steps forward. Often it takes two resets to make it to the line. That is normal. Patience through the transition zone is a skill in its own right.

Reset at the kitchen line

When an opponent speeds up at you at the line, you have two choices: counter-attack or reset. Counter-attacking works when the ball is high and you are balanced. When it is at your hip or lower, or you are stretched, a reset is usually better: soft block, short ball, back to dinking. The attacker loses the initiative they spent the speed-up to gain.

Common mistakes

  1. Swinging. Any real backswing adds pace. Keep the stroke short.
  2. Tight grip. The ball flies long.
  3. Moving through the shot. Split-step first.
  4. Aiming too close to the net. A reset into the tape is worse than a reset a bit deep. Give the net a foot of clearance.
  5. Standing up. Bending at the waist instead of the knees closes the paddle face.

Drills

Drive and reset (two people)
One player at the baseline drives medium-pace balls; the other stands in the transition zone and resets every ball into the kitchen. Twenty balls, then swap. Increase the pace as it gets easier.
Reset and advance (two people)
Start at the baseline. Your partner at the line feeds hard balls at your feet. Reset, take two steps, reset again, until you reach the line.
Speed-up and block (two people)
Both at the line, dinking. Either player may speed up at any time; the other must reset, not counter. Play to 11.
Wall block (alone)
Stand close to a wall and hit hard, then soft-block the rebound back to a line at net height. Trains grip pressure.

Common questions

What is a reset in pickleball?
A soft shot hit from a defensive position, usually against a hard ball, that lands in the opponents’ kitchen so the rally returns to a neutral dink exchange.
When should I reset instead of attacking?
When the ball is below net height, when you are off balance or moving, or when you are in the transition zone. Attack only high balls from a balanced position.
How do I stop my resets from going too high?
Split-step before contact so you are not moving forward, bend your knees, loosen your grip, and use almost no backswing.
Is a reset the same as a dink?
The stroke is similar. The difference is the situation: a reset is played under pressure against pace, a dink is part of a slow exchange.
