Serve and return strategy

The first two shots of every point: where to put them, what they are for, and the small choices that decide who gets to the kitchen line first. Updated 27 September 2026.

The serve and the return are the two shots every player hits in every point, and they are the two shots most players think about least. The serve feels like a formality because it is underhand; the return feels like just getting the ball back. Both are wrong. The serve decides how much time the receiver has; the return decides whether the serving team can get to the net at all. Neither is likely to win the point on its own, but either one can lose it, and together they set up everything that happens next.

What each shot is for

Start with the two-bounce rule, because it defines the job of both shots. The serve has to bounce, and so does the return. That means the receiving team can walk up to the kitchen line after the return, while the serving team has to wait at the baseline for the third ball. So the point begins with a built-in advantage for the receivers.

  • The serve’s job is to shrink that advantage: keep the receiver deep, give them as little time as possible to reach the line, and never give away a free point by missing.
  • The return’s job is to cash the advantage in: keep the serving team pinned at the baseline and buy enough time to get to the kitchen line before the third shot arrives.

Everything below follows from those two sentences.

The serve

Depth first, always

A deep serve lands in the back third of the service box and makes the receiver hit from on or behind the baseline. From there the return has 44 feet to travel before it reaches your side, and the receiver has 37 feet to run to reach the kitchen line. That is a long way. A short serve, landing near the kitchen line, lets the receiver step in, hit from inside the court, and reach the line before you have even hit your third shot.

Depth also beats pace. A slow serve that lands a foot from the baseline is harder to attack than a fast one that lands in the middle of the box, because pace can be redirected and depth cannot. If you only improve one thing about your serve, make it land deeper.

Never miss

You get one attempt. A missed serve ends the point with no rally, and in doubles it also burns one of your team’s two servers. That is a heavy price for trying to hit an ace in a sport where aces are rare. A reasonable target at recreational level is 95 percent of serves in. If you are missing more than one in twenty, slow down and aim for the middle of the box’s back third until the misses stop.

Where to aim

  1. Deep middle (toward the centreline, near the baseline). The safest serve. It is furthest from both sidelines so misses stay in, and it gives the receiver no angle to return with.
  2. Deep to the backhand. Most players’ backhand returns are shorter and weaker. For a right-handed receiver on the right side, that is the centreline corner; on the left side, it is the sideline corner.
  3. Deep and wide. Pulls the receiver off the court and makes them run further to reach the line. Riskier, because a miss wide is out.
  4. Short and wide, occasionally. A surprise short angle can catch a receiver standing deep. Use it rarely; it is the serve that gets attacked if they read it.

Spin and pace

Topspin makes a serve dip and kick up off the bounce, which pushes the receiver back; slice makes it skid low. Both are useful once you can put 95 percent in play. Pace is useful in the same way, but a hard serve that lands in the middle of the box comes back just as hard. Treat spin and pace as refinements on a deep serve, never as replacements for one. The serving rules limit how you can generate spin, notably the ban on spinning the ball with your fingers as you release it.

After the serve: stay back

Stand a step behind the baseline after serving, not on it. The return is coming deep, and you are not allowed to volley it. Players who creep forward after serving end up hitting the third ball off their shoelaces. Wait for the return to bounce, then hit your third, then move.

The return

Deep, again

The return has the same priority as the serve: depth. A return that lands within a few feet of the serving team’s baseline keeps them back, gives them a hard ball to drop, and gives you time to reach the line. A short return lets the serving team step in and either drive a low ball at you while you are still moving or drop from much closer, which is much easier.

High and slow is fine

This surprises tennis players. A higher, slower return is often better than a hard, flat one, because the slower the ball travels, the more time you have to walk to the kitchen line. A deep, loopy return that takes two seconds to arrive is a better return than a flat drive that takes one second and lands in the middle of the court. Hit through the ball with some lift and let it carry.

Get to the line, every time

The return is only half of the receiver’s job. The other half is the walk. As soon as the ball leaves your paddle, move forward to the kitchen line, and try to be set, feet still and paddle up, before the third shot is struck. If you are still moving when the third ball arrives, you will be hitting it off your feet from the transition zone, and the advantage you started with is gone. Players who hit good returns and stay at the baseline to admire them are giving away the most valuable thing the rules give them.

Where to aim the return

  • Deep middle. Makes the two servers decide who takes it, and gives them no angle.
  • Deep to the weaker player. If one opponent is clearly weaker, make them hit every third shot.
  • Deep to the server’s backhand. The server has just finished their motion and is often slightly off balance on the backhand side.
  • At the server’s feet if they creep in. A server who moves forward after serving can be punished with a deep return that lands at their feet.

Handling tricky serves

A very deep, kicking serve
Take a step back before the serve and let it come to you. Block it back deep with a short swing; do not try to attack it.
A short, angled serve
Move forward and take it early, then keep going to the line. It is a gift if you read it; you are now closer to the net than you normally would be.
A low, skidding slice serve
Bend your knees and lift it. A flat swing will put it in the net.
A serve aimed at your partner at the line
If it hits them before bouncing, you lose the point, so your partner should step out of the way. Some call this a Nasty Nelson; it is legal and rare.

Doubles positioning for the first two shots

Serving team: both players start at the baseline. The server’s partner stands beside them, not at the line, because the return will be deep and must bounce before either of you can hit it. Stay back until the third shot is hit.

Receiving team: the receiver stands at or just behind the baseline, ready for a deep serve. The receiver’s partner stands at the kitchen line from the start. The rules only require the ball to bounce, so there is nothing stopping the non-receiving partner from already being in the best position on the court. After the return, the receiver joins them.

Singles adjustments

In singles, there is no partner at the line, so the return needs to buy even more time. Return deep and to the corner that makes the server move furthest. On the serve, stand closer to the centreline so you can cover both sides of the return, and serve deep more than wide, because an angled return against a single player opens the whole court.

Common questions

Where should I aim my pickleball serve?
Deep, in the back third of the service box. The deep middle is the safest target; deep to the backhand is the most effective. Depth matters far more than pace.
Should I serve hard in pickleball?
Only if you can keep it deep and still land 95 percent of serves. A missed serve loses the point outright, and pace alone is easy to redirect.
Where should I hit my return of serve?
Deep, close to the serving team’s baseline, ideally to the middle or the weaker player. A higher, slower return is fine if it is deep, because it gives you time to reach the kitchen line.
What should I do after returning serve?
Walk forward to the kitchen line straight away and try to be set before the third shot is hit. The two-bounce rule gives the receiving team the net; staying back wastes it.
Where should the server’s partner stand?
At the baseline beside the server. The return must bounce before the serving team can hit it, so standing at the line gains nothing.
Where should the receiver’s partner stand?
At the kitchen line, from the start of the point. Only the ball has to bounce; the partner can already be in position.

Keep going: the shot after the return is the third-shot drop, and the rule that shapes both of these shots is the two-bounce rule.
