Pickleball vs tennis vs padel vs paddle ball

Courts drawn to scale, equipment, serve, scoring, walls, and which sport people mean when they say “paddle ball.” Updated 21 September 2026.

From a distance every racket sport looks like the same thing: two or four people, a net, a ball going back and forth. Up close they are built on different rules, and the rules are what make each one feel the way it does. Pickleball is not small tennis, padel is not tennis with walls, and “paddle ball” is at least four different games depending on which country and which decade you are in. This page puts them side by side.

Pickleball vs tennis

The obvious differences are size and pace: a pickleball court is under a third the area of a tennis court, and the plastic ball comes off the paddle far slower than a felt ball off strings. But the differences that change how you play are the rules, not the dimensions.

  • The serve is not a weapon. Tennis points are shaped by the serve; a big server can win most of their service games on the serve alone. Pickleball serves are underhand, one attempt, and have to clear a 7-foot zone. Their job is to start the rally. Aces are rare and serving is, if anything, a slight disadvantage.
  • Nobody rushes the net behind the serve. The two-bounce rule makes the serving side let the return bounce, so serve-and-volley does not exist. The receiving side gets to the net first.
  • You cannot volley from the kitchen. The 7-foot non-volley zone has no equivalent in tennis. It stops a tall player standing at the net and putting away everything, and it creates the dink, the soft shot that lands in the zone and cannot be attacked in the air.
  • Scoring is simpler and stranger. Games to 11, win by 2, and in traditional play only the serving side can score. The scoring guide explains the three-number call that baffles tennis players.
  • Doubles is the main event. Tennis is a singles sport with a doubles sideline; pickleball is the reverse.

For tennis players the transition is fast in the first hour and slow after that. The hand-eye skill carries over; the instinct to hit hard and close the net does not, and it takes weeks to learn to wait at the line and dink.

Pickleball vs padel

Padel is the other paddle sport growing at a ridiculous rate, dominant in Spain, Argentina, Italy and Sweden and spreading everywhere else. It is closer to tennis than pickleball is: tennis scoring, a felt ball, a serve into a box with two attempts. What makes it its own sport is the cage. The court is enclosed in glass and mesh, and after the ball bounces on the court it can be played off the walls, like squash. Rallies are long because the walls keep the ball alive, and positioning is about angles off the glass.

The two are easy to tell apart on sight: padel has walls, pickleball has a plastic ball with holes. They also feel different. Padel rallies are long and looping; pickleball rallies are short and quiet at the kitchen line until someone speeds up. Both are easier to start than tennis and both are overwhelmingly doubles sports, which is why they have grown in the same social spaces.

What “paddle ball” means

This is where the naming gets messy, and it is worth untangling because the same two words describe several unrelated games.

Paddleball (American, wall game)
A one-wall, three-wall or four-wall court game with a solid wooden or composite paddle and a small rubber ball, related to handball and racquetball. Big in New York City playgrounds, where one-wall paddleball courts are everywhere. No net.
Beach paddle ball (frescobol, matkot, beach tennis)
Two people with wooden paddles keeping a ball in the air on a beach, cooperatively rather than competitively. In Brazil it is frescobol; in Israel matkot. Beach tennis is the competitive version with a net, played on sand with a depressurised ball.
Paddle tennis / pop tennis
A century-old American game on a shrunken tennis court (50 × 20 feet) with a solid paddle and a depressurised tennis ball, underhand serve. Rebranded as pop tennis in 2015. The closest historical relative of pickleball.
Platform tennis (“paddle”)
Played outdoors in winter on a raised, heated aluminium deck surrounded by taut wire screens that are in play, with a solid paddle and a sponge rubber ball. People in the northeastern US who say they “play paddle” mean this.
Padel
Often called paddle or paddle tennis in English-speaking countries, and “pádel” in Spanish. The enclosed-court sport above.
Pickleball
Increasingly what someone means when they search for a paddle ball game, because it is the one their neighbourhood park just painted lines for.

If you are trying to work out which one you have been invited to play: walls and glass mean padel, a wire cage on a heated deck means platform tennis, a plastic ball with holes means pickleball, a single wall and a rubber ball means paddleball, and a beach means it is not a competition.

Which is easiest to pick up?

Pickleball, and it is not close. The court is small, the ball is slow, the serve is underhand, and a first-timer can have a rally within ten minutes. Padel is next, because the walls forgive misses and the serve is easy, though learning the angles takes a while. Tennis is the hardest to start: the overhead serve alone takes most people months.

Easy to start is not the same as easy to master. Pickleball’s learning curve goes flat quickly and then rises again at the kitchen line, where the difference between a 3.5 player and a 4.5 player is almost entirely patience, hands and the third-shot drop. That is the part of the sport that a tennis background does not give you.

Which is the best workout?

Singles tennis, then padel, then pickleball doubles, in roughly that order of running. Pickleball singles is a real workout; doubles is a lot of short lateral movement and a lot of standing at the line, which is exactly why it works for people whose knees have retired from tennis. Injury patterns follow: tennis stresses shoulders and elbows, padel and pickleball produce more falls and calf strains from sudden short movements.

Common questions

Is pickleball just mini tennis?
No. The kitchen rule, the two-bounce rule and the underhand serve give it a completely different rhythm. Tennis players adapt fast to the hitting and slowly to the waiting.
Is padel the same as paddle tennis?
No. Padel is the enclosed glass-court sport; paddle tennis (now pop tennis) is an open-court American game. Padel is often called paddle in English, which causes the confusion.
Can I use a tennis racket for pickleball?
Not legally, and not usefully. Pickleball paddles are solid, strings are not permitted, and a tennis racket is far too long for the kitchen game.
Which has a bigger court, pickleball or badminton?
They are the same: 20 × 44 feet. Pickleball was first played on a badminton court.
Which sport is growing fastest?
Pickleball in North America, padel in Europe and Latin America. Both have roughly doubled their player numbers in a few years.
