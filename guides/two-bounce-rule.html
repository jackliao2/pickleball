The two-bounce rule

Serve bounces. Return bounces. Then volleys. The rule that decides how every pickleball point begins. Updated 21 September 2026.

If the kitchen is the rule that makes pickleball its own sport, the two-bounce rule is the one that makes every point start the same way. It is short, it is easy to state, and it quietly decides who is at the net and who is stuck at the back for the first three shots of every rally. Understand it and the third-shot drop, the sport’s signature shot, stops being a mystery.

The rule

After the serve, each side must let the ball bounce once before hitting it. In practice that means:

  1. The serve must bounce before the receiver hits it. The receiver may not volley the serve.
  2. The return of serve must bounce before the serving side hits it. The server (or the server’s partner) may not volley the return.
  3. From the third shot onwards, either side may volley, as long as they are not in the kitchen while doing it.

Notice that it is one bounce per side, not two bounces in a row. The ball bounces once on the receiver’s side (the serve) and once on the server’s side (the return). Those are the two bounces. A ball that bounces twice on the same side is a different thing entirely: that is a fault against the side that let it bounce twice.

Two-bounce or double-bounce?

Same rule, two names. It was called the double-bounce rule for decades and many players still say that. USA Pickleball changed the official name to the two-bounce rule in 2018 precisely because “double bounce” also describes the ball bouncing twice on one side, which is a fault. If someone calls “double bounce” during a game, check which one they mean.

Why the rule exists

Without it, the serving team could serve and sprint to the net, volleying the return before the receiver had a chance to get set. In tennis that is a legitimate strategy with a big serve behind it; in pickleball, where the serve is underhand and the court is 44 feet long, it would make serving an overwhelming advantage and turn every point into a race to the kitchen line. The two-bounce rule takes that away from the serving side in two steps.

First, the receiver cannot volley the serve, which stops the receiving team from standing at the kitchen line for the serve and killing anything short. The receiver has to start near the baseline, because a deep serve has to be played after the bounce.

Second, and more importantly, the serving team cannot volley the return. That pins them at the baseline for the third shot. The receiver, meanwhile, hits the return and walks straight to the kitchen line, joining their partner who was already there. By the time the third shot is hit, the receiving team has two players at the net and the serving team has two players 40 feet away. This is the fundamental imbalance of a pickleball point, and it is the reason serving is not an advantage in this sport.

What the receiving team should do

The rule gives the receiving team the net for free, so take it. The standard advice is return deep and move up. A deep return keeps the serving team at the baseline for their third shot and gives you time to reach the kitchen line before the ball comes back. A short return invites the server to hit a drive and follow it in, and hands back the advantage the rule gave you. Do not bother trying to hit a winner on the return. A ball that lands in the back third of the court and lets you get to the line is a good return.

The receiver’s partner does not have to wait for anything. They can stand at the kitchen line from the start, because they are not the one hitting the return. Only the ball has to bounce; the partner’s position is unrestricted.

What the serving team should do: the third-shot drop

The serving team’s problem on the third shot is that two opponents are at the net and anything hit at them hard and high is coming back harder and lower. Driving the third shot works sometimes, especially against players with slow hands, but at any decent level it is a losing proposition.

The solution the sport arrived at is the third-shot drop: a soft, arcing shot that lands in the opponents’ kitchen. It works because of the kitchen rule. A ball that lands in the kitchen cannot be volleyed; the receivers have to let it bounce, and after it bounces it is below net height and has to be lifted. While that ball is in the air the serving team walks forward. If the drop is good, they arrive at the kitchen line as the opponents are hitting up on a low ball, and the point is now even: four players at the line, a dink rally, the person who pops up first loses. The rule that took the net away has been neutralised by one shot.

Learning the drop is the single biggest step from beginner to intermediate. A bad drop, one that floats high or lands past the kitchen, is the shot you see get slammed. A good one is the shot you see and think nothing happened, because nothing did, which was the point.

The faults it creates

  • Volleying the serve. The receiver punches the serve out of the air. Fault, point to the serving side. This happens constantly with new players who have tennis reflexes; a short serve that looks attackable is a trap.
  • Volleying the return. The server or partner steps in and takes the return in the air. Fault. The common version is the server’s partner, standing near the baseline, reaching for a return down the middle before it bounces.
  • Charging the net behind the serve. Not a fault in itself, but you will arrive at the kitchen line as the return bounces behind you. The rule makes rushing pointless.

Under both side-out and rally scoring these are ordinary faults: the side that committed them loses the rally.

Common questions

Can the receiver volley the serve?
No. It is a fault.
Can the server’s partner volley the return?
No. The whole serving side has to let the return bounce.
Does the rule apply after the third shot?
No. From the third shot on, the only restriction on volleys is the kitchen rule.
Is the two-bounce rule the same as the double-bounce rule?
Yes, same rule. The name was changed in 2018 to avoid confusion with a ball bouncing twice on one side.
Can the receiver’s partner stand at the kitchen line during the serve?
Yes. Only the ball has to bounce; where players stand is up to them.
Why do people say serving is a disadvantage in pickleball?
Because of this rule. The receiving team gets to the net first, and under side-out scoring the serving team also has to win the rally just to score.
