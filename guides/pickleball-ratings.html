Pickleball ratings explained

What 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, 4.0 and 4.5 actually mean on court, how to self-rate without lying, and how DUPR and UTPR work. Updated 21 September 2026.

Sooner or later someone at open play asks “what are you?” and expects a number. The number is a skill rating, and pickleball uses them more than almost any other recreational sport: to sort open-play courts, to seed tournaments, to run leagues, and, on some Saturdays, to decide who is allowed on the good court. This page explains what the levels mean in terms of shots you can hit, how to place yourself honestly, and how the two big results-based systems, DUPR and UTPR, turn matches into a number with three decimal places.

The skill levels

The scale runs from 1.0 to about 6.0 in half-steps. USA Pickleball publishes definitions for each level; the table below is a plain-English version of what you can actually expect from a player at each one. The honest test for any level is not “can I do this sometimes” but “can I do this most of the time under pressure.”

LevelWhat they can do
1.0–2.0Brand new. Learning the rules, the serve, and how to keep a ball in play. Has heard of the kitchen.
2.5Knows the rules. Can serve and return, sustain a short rally, and keep score. Volleys are hit-or-miss; the soft game does not exist yet; every third shot is a drive or a lob.
3.0Consistent serves and returns, medium-pace rallies from the baseline, and a working forehand. Knows what a dink is and can hit one in a drill; in a game it is inconsistent. Third-shot drop is attempted and often too high. Understands positioning but cannot always get there.
3.5The big step. Third-shot drop lands in the kitchen more often than not. Can sustain a dink rally and is starting to dink with a target. Gets to the kitchen line and stays there. Uses a backhand. Beginning to vary pace and to choose drive vs drop. Still pops up under pressure and still speeds up the wrong ball.
4.0Consistent soft game. Drops, dinks and resets under pressure; rarely pops up. Attacks the right ball and knows when to be patient. Moves well with a partner, communicates, stacks when useful, covers the middle. Beginning to hit with spin and to use the Erne and ATP on occasion.
4.5Very few unforced errors. Every shot in the book, hit with intent. Reads opponents, forces the pattern, wins hands battles. Can drive and drop from both wings and turn defence into offence with a reset. Competitive in regional tournaments.
5.0+Tournament and professional level. Everything above, faster, plus the ability to change tactics mid-match and expose small weaknesses. Pro tour players are typically rated 6.0 and above on DUPR.

Most active recreational players sit between 3.0 and 3.5, and the crowd thins quickly above 4.0. If you are unsure, you are probably a half-step lower than you hope. That is not an insult; it is how self-rating works everywhere.

How to self-rate honestly

Ratings are supposed to describe what you do in a game, not in a drill. Some questions that separate the levels:

  • 2.5 vs 3.0: Can you get 8 out of 10 serves in deep? Can you hit a return and get to the line before the third shot arrives?
  • 3.0 vs 3.5: In a real game, do your third-shot drops land in the kitchen more than half the time? Can you dink 10 in a row without one sitting up?
  • 3.5 vs 4.0: When someone speeds up at you, do you block it softly into the kitchen or does it fly? Do you attack balls above the net and leave the ones below it?
  • 4.0 vs 4.5: Do you win most of the hands battles you are in? Do you go whole games without an unforced error on a dink?

A useful rule: you are the level at which you are an average player on the court, not the level at which you can occasionally hang. If you are the best player in your 3.5 group and the worst in the 4.0 group, you are a 3.5 who is about to be a 4.0.

Results-based ratings

Self-ratings drift, so competitive play uses ratings calculated from match results. Two matter.

DUPR

DUPR (Dynamic Universal Pickleball Rating) is the most widely used. Everyone, from a first-timer to a touring pro, is on one scale, roughly 2.0 to 8.0, and the rating is recalculated after every match that gets recorded, whether that is a sanctioned tournament, a club league, or a recreational game both sides agree to log. The algorithm looks at three things: who you played (their ratings), whether you won, and by how much. Beat a stronger team and your number goes up; beat a much weaker team 11–9 and it can go down, because you were expected to win by more. Singles and doubles are rated separately. New players start with a provisional rating that firms up over a couple of dozen matches, and a rating with few results behind it is noisy and should be treated that way.

DUPR numbers do not line up exactly with the self-rating scale above, but as a loose guide a 3.5 self-rated player is often somewhere between about 3.25 and 3.75 on DUPR, and the pro tour operates above 6.0. Leagues and tournaments increasingly use DUPR brackets (for example “under 3.5”) instead of self-declared levels for exactly the reason self-ratings exist: people are bad at self-rating.

UTPR

UTPR (USA Pickleball Tournament Player Rating) is USA Pickleball’s own rating, built only from sanctioned tournament results, shown to two decimal places, and used to seed and place players in USA Pickleball events. It is separate by event type (men’s doubles, mixed, singles). If you do not play sanctioned tournaments you do not have a meaningful one.

Others

Various apps, leagues and clubs run their own ladders and ratings, and some rec centres do in-person skill assessments. They are all measuring the same thing with less data. When two ratings disagree, the one with more recent matches behind it is usually closer to the truth.

Why your rating moves the way it does

It dropped after a win
You were expected to win by more. Margin counts.
It rose after a loss
You lost narrowly to a team rated well above you. The algorithm treats that as evidence you are better than your number.
It jumps around a lot
Not enough matches yet, or you are playing with wildly different partners. Doubles ratings reflect the team; a strong partner drags you up and a weak one down, in the short run.
It is stuck
You are playing the same people at the same level and winning about half. That is what a stable rating looks like. To move it, play up.

How to move up a level

The gaps are not the same size, and they are not about power.

  • 2.5 → 3.0: Consistency. Serve deep, return deep, stop hitting the ball out. Learn the rules cold, including the two-bounce rule and the kitchen, so you stop giving away free points.
  • 3.0 → 3.5: The third-shot drop and the dink. This is the soft game, and it is the whole gap. Get to the line, stay there, keep the ball low.
  • 3.5 → 4.0: Shot selection and hands. Speed up only the high ball. Block the speed-up back softly. Reset from the transition zone instead of swinging. Play with a regular partner and talk.
  • 4.0 → 4.5: Fewer errors than the other team, in every match, and the ability to change what is not working. This one takes years and usually a coach.

Common questions

What is the average pickleball rating?
Most recreational players are between 3.0 and 3.5. Players above 4.0 are a small minority.
What rating is a beginner?
1.0 to 2.5, depending on whether they know the rules and can sustain a rally.
Do I need a DUPR to play tournaments?
Many tournaments and leagues now require or use one for brackets. Sanctioned USA Pickleball events use UTPR. Open play usually uses self-ratings.
Are singles and doubles rated separately?
Yes, in both DUPR and UTPR. Many players are a half-level different between the two.
Can I rate myself 4.0 if I was a good tennis player?
Not yet. Tennis skills transfer to hitting; the soft game does not transfer at all. Most strong tennis players start around 3.0 and move up quickly.
